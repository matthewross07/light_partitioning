Ternary Partitioning of Continental-Scale Water Clarity

Section 3 Partitioning Non-Algal Particles (NAP) & Method Sensitivity

The additive Beer–Lambert model partitions non-water light attenuation among three mutually exclusive constituent pools: phytoplankton cells, non-algal suspended particles (NAP, comprising mineral sediments, organic detritus, and depigmented cell fragments), and dissolved organic matter (represented by DOC). Because standard gravimetric monitoring measures bulk Total Suspended Solids (TSS)—which includes both non-algal particles and living phytoplankton biomass—living algal dry mass must be subtracted from bulk TSS to isolate NAP:

Optical Component Measured In-Situ Variable Model Predictor & Units
Phytoplankton (\(\alpha_{\text{Chl }a}\,\text{Chl }a\)) Chlorophyll a (\(\mu\text{g L}^{-1}\)) \(\text{Chl }a\) (\(\text{mg m}^{-3} = \mu\text{g L}^{-1}\)) used directly
Non-algal particles (\(\alpha_{\text{NAP}}\,\text{NAP}\)) Bulk TSS / SSC (\(\text{mg L}^{-1}\)) \(\text{NAP} = \text{TSS} - \text{biomass}(\text{Chl }a)\) (\(\text{g m}^{-3} = \text{mg L}^{-1}\))
Dissolved organic matter (\(\alpha_{\text{DOC}}\,\text{DOC}\)) DOC (\(\text{mg L}^{-1}\)) \(\text{DOC}\) (\(\text{g m}^{-3} = \text{mg L}^{-1}\)) used directly

Following the empirical bio-optical relationship of Gohin et al. (2005) (Remote Sens. Environ. 95:29–46), phytoplankton dry biomass (in \(\text{mg L}^{-1}\), converting \(\mu\text{g L}^{-1}\) to \(\text{mg L}^{-1}\) via division by \(1{,}000\)) is estimated from \(\text{Chl }a\) (\(\mu\text{g L}^{-1}\)) as a nonlinear power law:

\[\text{biomass}_{\text{algal}}\ [\text{mg L}^{-1}] = \frac{234}{1000}\,(\text{Chl }a)^{0.57} = 0.234\,(\text{Chl }a)^{0.57}, \qquad \text{NAP} = \text{TSS} - 0.234\,(\text{Chl }a)^{0.57}\]

In code, add_nap() in R/prep.R computes chl_a_biomass and stores non-algal particles in the column tss_dead.

Key Bio-Optical Decisions: NAP Derivation (R/prep.R)

  • Why \(\text{Chl }a\) is retained as the regression predictor while algal dry mass is subtracted from TSS. Algal dry mass is computed solely to remove living phytoplankton mass from bulk gravimetric TSS. Using \(\text{Chl }a\) (\(\text{mg m}^{-3}\)) directly as the third predictor in the linear regression (\(k_d - k_w = \alpha_{\text{NAP}}\,\text{NAP} + \alpha_{\text{DOC}}\,\text{DOC} + \alpha_{\text{Chl }a}\,\text{Chl }a\)) ensures that the fitted slope \(\alpha_{\text{Chl }a}\) has standard bio-optical units of \(\text{m}^2\ (\text{mg Chl }a)^{-1}\), enabling direct comparison with theoretical first-principles chlorophyll cross-sections (\(k_c \approx 0.008\text{–}0.020\text{ m}^2\ [\text{mg Chl }a]^{-1}\); Bannister 1974; Kirk 1994).
  • Why the nonlinear Gohin et al. (2005) power law (\(0.234\,\text{Chl }a^{0.57}\)) outperforms constant linear C:Chl ratios. Intracellular carbon-to-chlorophyll ratios decline systematically from oligotrophic to eutrophic waters due to nutrient sufficiency and self-shading photoacclimation (Geider et al. 1987, 1997; Cloern et al. 1995; Gohin et al. 2005). As demonstrated in the sensitivity table below, constant linear dry-mass:Chl a ratios (100:1 or 200:1) severely over-predict algal dry mass during high-chlorophyll blooms, generating up to 31.7% non-physical negative NAP values, whereas the Gohin et al. (2005) power law produces negative NAP in only 2.02% of samples while yielding virtually identical attenuation slopes.
  • Handling of near-zero negative NAP in training vs. application. During OLS model training (n = 275,349), the 1.66% (4,580 rows) of samples where \(0.234\,\text{Chl }a^{0.57}\) slightly exceeds measured TSS are retained without truncation so random analytical noise near zero does not bias slope estimates. When projecting individual samples into the \([0, 1]^3\) ternary simplex (apply_model7()), records are required to have strictly positive \(\text{NAP} > 0.01\text{ mg L}^{-1}\) so every sample’s three attenuation fractions lie strictly inside \([0, 1]\).

3.1 Sensitivity to the Algal Biomass Conversion Formulation

To verify that our continental conclusions are insensitive to the choice of algal biomass conversion, we compare the nonlinear Gohin et al. (2005) power law (\(0.234\,\text{Chl }a^{0.57}\)) against constant linear dry-mass:Chl a ratios of 50:1, 100:1, and 200:1:

ratios <- tibble(a = P$chl_ratio_sensitivity)
neg <- ratios %>% mutate(res = map(a, ~ add_nap(get_simul('no_secchi'), a = .x) %>%
                                     summarize(pct_negative_nap = mean(tss_dead < 0) * 100))) %>%
  unnest(res)
tr <- get_training(); k_w <- get_k_w()
slopes <- ratios %>% mutate(fit = map(a, function(a) {
  d <- add_nap(tr %>% select(-chl_ratio, -power, -chl_a_biomass, -tss_dead), a = a)
  m <- lm((kd - k_w) ~ 0 + tss_dead + doc + chl_a, data = d)
  tibble(NAP = coef(m)[1], DOC = coef(m)[2], Chl_a = coef(m)[3],
         R2 = cor(d$kd, fitted(m))^2)
})) %>% unnest(fit)
left_join(neg, slopes, by = 'a') %>%
  mutate(form = ifelse(a == 234, '0.234 × Chl^0.57 (Gohin et al. 2005; canonical)',
                       paste0(a / 1000, ' × Chl (constant linear ', a, ':1)'))) %>%
  select(form, pct_negative_nap, NAP, DOC, Chl_a, R2) %>%
  kable(digits = 4, col.names = c('Algal Dry-Biomass Formulation (mg/L)', '% Samples with NAP < 0', 'α_NAP (m²/g)', 'α_DOC (m²/g)', 'α_Chl a (m²/mg)', 'Global R²')) %>%
  kable_styling(bootstrap_options = c('condensed', 'striped'), full_width = FALSE)

0.05 × Chl (constant linear 50:1) 0.7343 0.0431 0.0720 0.0095 0.4077

Notice a fundamental algebraic property of linear regressions: under any constant linear conversion (\(\text{biomass} = r\,\text{Chl }a\)), \(\alpha_{\text{NAP}}\) (0.0448) and \(\alpha_{\text{DOC}}\) (0.0694) are mathematically identical across all values of \(r\) because \(\text{TSS} - r\,\text{Chl }a\) is a linear combination of \(\text{TSS}\) and \(\text{Chl }a\), shifting only \(\alpha_{\text{Chl }a}\) by \(+r\,\alpha_{\text{NAP}}\). The nonlinear Gohin et al. (2005) power law minimizes negative NAP artifacts (2.02%) while keeping \(\alpha_{\text{Chl }a} = 0.0089\text{ m}^2\text{ mg}^{-1}\) squarely within the theoretical Bannister (1974) / Kirk (1994) range (\(0.008\text{–}0.020\text{ m}^2\text{ mg}^{-1}\)).

3.2 Sensitivity to Analytical Method Tiers

The AquaMatch datasets classify analytical laboratory and field methods into harmonized confidence tiers (tier 1 = strictest standard methods; tier 2/3 = broader comparable methods; Brousil et al. 2024a, 2024b, 2025; De La Torre et al. 2025). Fitting the global model across all harmonized tiers vs. restricting strictly to top-tier records confirms that pooling all harmonized tiers nearly triples spatial coverage while leaving the fitted specific attenuation coefficients virtually unchanged:

tr_strict <- tr %>% filter(chl_tier == 1, doc_tier == 1, tss_tier == 1, sdd_tier <= 2)
m_all    <- coef(lm((kd - k_w) ~ 0 + tss_dead + doc + chl_a, data = tr))
m_strict <- coef(lm((kd - k_w) ~ 0 + tss_dead + doc + chl_a, data = tr_strict))
tibble(
  Subset = c('All harmonized AquaMatch tiers (canonical training set)',
             'Strict top analytical tiers only (Tier 1 chemistry, Tier 1–2 Secchi)'),
  `Observations (n)` = fmt_n(c(nrow(tr), nrow(tr_strict))),
  `Unique Sites` = fmt_n(c(n_distinct(tr$SiteID), n_distinct(tr_strict$SiteID))),
  `α_NAP (m²/g)` = c(m_all[1], m_strict[1]),
  `α_DOC (m²/g)` = c(m_all[2], m_strict[2]),
  `α_Chl a (m²/mg)` = c(m_all[3], m_strict[3])
) %>% kable(digits = 4) %>% kable_styling(bootstrap_options = c('condensed', 'striped'), full_width = FALSE)
