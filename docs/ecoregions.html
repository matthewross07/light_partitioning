Ternary Partitioning of Continental-Scale Water Clarity

Section 5 Hierarchical Ecoregion Stratification (ecoregions.R)

A gram of dissolved organic carbon or suspended sediment does not attenuate the exact same amount of light everywhere. Humic DOC drained from northern peatlands is darker and more aromatic than autochthonous DOC in arid reservoirs (Spencer et al. 2012; Griffin et al. 2018), and fine clay suspensions scatter light much more efficiently per unit mass than coarse glacial silt (Bowers et al. 2020).

To capture this regional biogeochemical coherence across streams, lakes, and estuaries within a single continental framework, eco_join() in R/ecoregions.R assigns each monitoring station (SiteID) to its nested U.S. EPA / CEC North American Ecoregion across Levels I–IV (Omernik & Griffith 2014). Nearshore estuarine stations located slightly seaward of coastline polygons are matched to the nearest coastal ecoregion via sf::st_nearest_feature(), guaranteeing a strict 1-to-1 mapping from SiteID to ecoregion.

Figure 5.1: Left: U.S. EPA Level III Ecoregions (NA_L3CODE). Right: Top 18 Level III ecoregions ranked by number of simultaneous training matchups, colored by water-body type.

5.1 Minimum Sample-Size Rule (zone_min = 100)

Every regional model requires at least 100 training matchups (P$zone_min = 100) to estimate an ecoregion’s parameters independently. At Level III (Model 7), 47 ecoregions exceed 100 matchups, covering 99.69% of all continental training data; the remaining 0.31% of observations in sparsely sampled ecoregions fall back to the continental model:
